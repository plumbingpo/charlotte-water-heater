A gas water heater is a fairly simple machine, which is part of why it lasts. A burner under the tank heats the water, exhaust rises through a flue that runs up the center of the tank, and a gas control valve on the front acts as both thermostat and safety shutoff. That valve only lets gas reach the burner while it senses a flame, through a thermocouple on standing-pilot models or a flame sensor on electronic ignition models. When something goes wrong, it is usually at one of those points. In Charlotte, NC, we regularly find thermocouples that no longer hold the valve open, pilots starved by a lint-clogged flame arrestor screen, gas valves that have failed internally, and flue or vent problems that let exhaust spill back into the room.

Diagnosing a gas water heater properly means checking combustion, not just parts. Across Charlotte, our technicians measure thermocouple output in millivolts, study the pilot and burner flame for color and shape, read gas pressure at the valve, and test draft at the hood so we know exhaust is leaving the house. We check the combustion chamber for soot and scorching, which point to a blocked flue or poor combustion air, and we test for carbon monoxide near the heater. Safety always comes first: a gas smell means leaving the house and calling the gas utility from outside before anything else, and Charlotte Water Heater can take over from there.

Some gas heaters need a quick part swap, and some need an honest conversation. We handle both. Thermocouples, flame sensors, pilot assemblies, hot surface igniters, gas valves, and burner cleaning are routine repairs, and we correct venting faults and flush sediment during the same visit when needed. If the tank is leaking, rusting through, or past about ten to twelve years with a major failure, we will explain why replacement makes more sense. Our installations cover like-for-like swaps and upgrades to power vent or high-efficiency models, with correct gas line sizing, sediment traps, expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic strapping wherever required. When to Call for Gas Water Heater Services

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you money and keep a slow drip from turning into a flooded utility room. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • A vent pipe that has separated, rusted, or left scorch marks near the top of the heater
  • Leaks at the drain valve, the fittings, or the bottom of the tank
  • Hot water that runs out faster than it used to
  • A rotten-egg odor near the water heater, in which case get out and call the gas company first
  • Loud rumbling from the bottom of the tank
  • Soot or scorch marks around the burner access door
  • No hot water at all, with the burner never firing
  • A pilot light that will not stay lit

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Two things make gas water heater work a poor DIY project: the parts involve fuel and combustion, and the symptoms overlap. A pilot outage might come from a worn thermocouple, a partly blocked pilot tube, lint on the flame arrestor screen, a downdraft, or an internal valve failure. A wrong guess there just wastes money. Guess wrong on a gas fitting or vent connection and the stakes are much higher, since a small leak or a spillage problem can put gas or carbon monoxide into the living space. Gas valve and heater replacements also typically call for a permit.

Our technicians approach gas heaters with instruments rather than assumptions. They check thermocouple output in millivolts, read gas pressure at the valve, test draft at the hood, check for CO around the appliance, and leak-test every joint they touch. That process fixes the actual fault the first time and leaves your home safer than before. We also give you an honest assessment of the heater itself. A tank past about ten or twelve years with a failing valve or a leak is usually a replacement, and we will say so before you spend money on a repair.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Follow these tips from our technicians:

  • Make sure the vent pipe stays connected, sloped upward, and free of rust
  • Give the heater breathing room and keep flammables well away from it
  • Keep the air intake at the bottom of the heater free of lint and dust
  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
  • Install carbon monoxide alarms on every level of the home and test them monthly
  • Check the anode rod every three to five years, since it protects the tank from corrosion
  • Drain sediment from the bottom of the tank once or twice a year
